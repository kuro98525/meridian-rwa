T+1 is why your money is stuck

A day of settlement lag sounds small. Across a portfolio, it's the hidden tax you've never seen on a statement.

5 min readUpdated Nov 2026

When you sell a stock, the trade happens instantly. The money, though, arrives tomorrow. That one-day gap has a name — T+1 settlement — and for most of your life you've accepted it as natural. It isn't. It's friction, and it's costing you more than you think.

For a single trade, a day is nothing. For a portfolio, it compounds into something real: capital that's committed but not available, opportunities missed because the proceeds hadn't landed, positions held longer than they should be because the exit takes until tomorrow.

Where the friction actually lives

The lag isn't the market's fault. It's the plumbing — the chain of custodians, clearinghouses, and counterparties that a trade has to pass through before ownership is finally, officially, settled. Each hop adds a check, and each check adds a day.

For listed securities, the industry has squeezed this down to T+1. For private credit and real estate, the lag isn't a day — it's weeks of paperwork, and then a lock-up measured in years. The same underlying value, but with no way to move it.

The asset wasn't the problem. The exit was.

What on-chain changes

On-chain settlement doesn't add a faster horse to the same carriage — it removes the carriage. Ownership is recorded directly, transfer is the settlement, and it happens in seconds, at any hour. Not "the next business day." Not "when the office opens." Now.

That's the entire unlock. The treasury, the real estate, the private credit — they were never bad assets. They were slow assets. Tokenization makes them fast, and speed is liquidity.

The honest frame

None of this changes the underlying risk. A treasury is still a treasury; private credit still has default risk. What changes is the clock — and for anyone who's ever watched a good exit slip away because the money couldn't move, that clock was the whole problem.
