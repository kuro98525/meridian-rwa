Private credit's illiquidity trap

The yield was never the problem. The lock-up was. Here's what's changing.

5 min readUpdated Nov 2026

Private credit pays more than public bonds, and everyone knows why: you're being compensated for giving up the ability to leave. That trade — yield for liquidity — was the only version on offer for decades. It no longer is.

The asset class exploded as banks retreated from lending after the financial crisis, leaving a gap that private funds filled. Attractive yields, senior-secured structures, floating rates. The catch was always the same: your money is locked up for years.

The lock-up was the price of entry

In a traditional private credit fund, you commit capital, and it's drawn down over time. You can't redeem when you want. Your exit is the fund's exit — three, five, seven years out, on the manager's schedule, not yours.

For institutions with patient capital, that's fine. For everyone else, it's the reason a perfectly good asset stayed out of reach. You didn't avoid private credit because the returns were bad. You avoided it because you couldn't get out.

You didn't avoid the asset. You avoided the lock-up.

What tokenization does

Record the ownership interest on-chain and the same exposure becomes divisible and tradable. The underlying loan book doesn't change. The credit risk doesn't change. What changes is that your position can now be moved — in pieces, on your schedule, without asking a fund manager for permission.

That single change turns a "locked" allocation into a liquid one. Not because the asset got safer, but because the exit finally exists.

The honest caveat

Liquidity on-chain is real, but it's not infinite — in a stress event, buyers can vanish just like in any market. And the underlying risk is unchanged: these are still loans, and loans default. What tokenization removes is the structural lock-up, not the risk. For an allocator, that's the difference between "can't" and "won't."
